## Why I Built Another Reminder App

I had tried enough productivity tools to know the market was crowded. Reminder apps, calendars, task managers, Kanban boards, habit tools - I have used some version of all of them for decades.

The frustrating part was that many of them tried to cover everything, then still fell apart around the small moments that decide whether a reminder actually gets handled. One app can show the notification but make <KeywordBadge>Bulk reminder management</KeywordBadge> painful. Another can support bulk changes but give you weak control over what happens when those reminders interrupt you. Some handle recurring rules but make snoozing feel rigid. Some are good at capture and weak at resolution.

A reminder should be the smallest possible interruption, but mine usually felt like a chore: unlock the phone, open the app, find the reminder, reschedule it, and hope the new time sticks. Even with AI moving quickly, <KeywordBadge>reminder follow-through</KeywordBadge> still feels early. The tools have not fully caught up to the real mess of notifications, recurring tasks, bulk changes, and attention switching.

## From Tap Flow To Voice Agent

The app still starts with the obvious path: create the reminder, see it at the right time, then handle it without rebuilding the whole plan.

Of course, most people start with taps. That still has to work. But after using enough general AI agents, I kept wanting a simpler product question: what if the reminder app had a <KeywordBadge>Voice agent</KeywordBadge> that could do the real reminder work? Most general agents are too broad for this moment, and most productivity reminder apps still stop before agentic actions get deep. That is the lane I explored and implemented.

Oakminder turns a typed, tapped, or spoken request into a real reminder: title, date, time, category, recurrence, snooze behavior, notification schedule, alarm behavior, Live Activity timing, and account state. When the reminder fires, the next action should stay cheap. Snooze it. Complete it. Defer it. Keep it visible until it is actually handled.

## Notification Actions Had To Carry More

The main design decision was to treat the notification as part of the reminder workflow, not a delivery detail.

Most reminder apps already support some version of <KeywordBadge>Notification actions</KeywordBadge>. The harder part was making the tap behave like real account state, not a local button on one phone. Oakminder tries to keep the decision where the interruption already happened. If a reminder fires while I am walking, cooking, driving, or in the middle of another task, I should be able to buy the right amount of time without searching through screens.

Custom snooze is the clearest example. The app supports presets, but real life does not always fit five, ten, or fifteen minutes. A meeting might end in 22 minutes. Laundry might need 91. I wanted control without ceremony.

## The Architecture Behind A Simple Tap

One of the hardest parts of Oakminder sits behind the simple action.

In plain English, it is the shared source of truth for reminders, recurrence, completion history, devices, notification scheduling, premium access, and AI credits. That was the architecture decision: the backend has to know what happened, and the phone has to make the action feel light.

## Voice Became The Action Layer

AI made sense in Oakminder only if it reduced work.

I did not want the voice feature to become a chatbot panel sitting beside the real app. A reminder app already has a natural input method: say the thing you need to remember. The best version was the one that changed reminder state the user could review.

That choice came from contrast with YouSoul, a different web app I had already built where AI agents communicated and did work through chat. If the job was remembering something while I was in motion, voice was the shorter bridge between intent and action.

## Onboarding Had To Earn Trust

Building a mobile app also taught me that onboarding is part of the product.

Good mobile onboarding does more than show welcome screens. It explains the product, teaches only what the user needs right now, and moves them into one useful action. For a reminder app, that action is simple: create a reminder, understand why notifications matter, and see how the app will help when the reminder comes back.

Mobile apps ask for trust quickly. <BrandIconLink provider="apple" label="Apple onboarding guidance" href="https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding" /> and <BrandIconLink provider="android" label="Android onboarding guidance" href="https://developer.android.com/design/ui/mobile/guides/patterns/onboarding" /> both point to the same product lesson: explain the value before asking for commitment. Android's notification-permission docs make <KeywordBadge>Permission timing</KeywordBadge> even more concrete: ask in context, when the user understands what the notifications are for. That matched what I was learning. Notification permission should not feel like a random system prompt. The app has to explain why it needs that permission, then ask near the moment where the user understands the value. The same is true for AI and voice-agent features in general: explain what the agent can actually do, let the user see the value, then make the subscription boundary feel connected to that value.

I was studying a lot from indie and startup mobile app founders during this period, especially how they handled onboarding and pricing surfaces. I kept seeing the same lesson from different angles: onboarding can hook the user, make monetization feel earned, and help the product sustain usage over a longer period. For Oakminder, that made onboarding part of the product work, not a screen I could leave until the end.

## Saving The Visual Pattern

For one piece of content, manual notes are enough. At volume, inspiration needs structure.

The trace pipeline is where the distribution studio starts to feel different from a folder of saved links. I wanted each trace to preserve the surroundings of an idea: creator context, caption or description, transcript, music context, frames, screenshots, text in frames, and <KeywordBadge>traceable IDs</KeywordBadge>. For slideshows and videos, the visible text is part of the creative pattern, so I used <BrandIconLink provider="google" label="Google Cloud Vision" href="https://cloud.google.com/vision/docs/ocr" /> for text recognition and <KeywordBadge>bounding-box extraction</KeywordBadge>. That gave me the words on the frame plus their placement, space, and layout role.

For content creation, the useful signal is often visual. Where does the hook sit? How much text is on the slide? Which frame sells the idea? What is the rhythm between caption, overlay, image, and CTA? The system does not need to perfectly clone a source. It needs enough context and visual structure to help a human creator, a generated creator-style draft, or an internal edit stay grounded in a real format.

The same <KeywordBadge>Context extraction</KeywordBadge> path feeds the creative side. Once the system has the product angle, source notes, transcript, frame sequence, visual text, and layout cues, it can help build a slideshow draft, rewrite a caption, brief a UGC partner, or give a generative workflow better inputs than "make a productivity ad." The point is to keep the useful parts of the inspiration alive as it moves through the studio.

## Creative Studio

Once research becomes structured context, the next question is production.

The studio needed a place where product angle, source notes, and extracted inspiration could turn into an <KeywordBadge>editable asset</KeywordBadge>. The slideshow editor became the main surface for that. It supports drafts, templates, post mode, inspiration mode, autosave, recovery, slide reorder, generated content, text overlays, image history, per-slide image generation, selected account context, sounds, speed, aspect ratio, CTA presets, content ideas, trace-agent context, caption rewrite, export state, and detail-sheet metadata.

That sounds heavy because the work is heavy. A draft that cannot be edited, reviewed, and traced back to a product angle is just a generated asset. I wanted the draft to keep its memory: why it exists, which product angle it uses, where the source format came from, which account or creator style it belongs to, which visual pattern it borrowed, and how it should leave the editor.

The studio widened from there. <BrandIconLink provider="remotion" label="Remotion" href="https://www.remotion.dev" /> gave video scenes a rendering path. The <KeywordBadge>hook factory</KeywordBadge> separated hook discovery and CTA production from final slideshow editing. Asset workflows kept local images, generated images, CTA clips, sounds, and replacement media available. Mobile queues made review possible away from the desktop.

## UGC Briefs And AI-Assisted Drafts

The distribution studio had to support more than one way to produce content.

Some app teams work with real UGC creators. That means the founder needs <KeywordBadge>UGC briefs</KeywordBadge>, examples, product context, do and do-not guidance, hooks, source patterns, tone, and review notes. A creator should not have to guess what the product does or why the user cares.

Other founders use <KeywordBadge>GenAI creator drafts</KeywordBadge> for their own channels. That path still needs context. A generative workflow is much stronger when it has the product angle, target audience, source format, transcript, frame structure, caption direction, CTA, and visual notes.

I built the studio so the same inputs could serve both modes. The system can collect inspiration, extract context, preserve the product angle, and turn the result into a brief, a slideshow draft, a caption rewrite, or an AI-generation input.

## What I Would Build Again

The part I would keep is the pairing.

Saturated markets still leave room for a product when the lived friction is real. I did not build Oakminder because reminder apps were useless. I built it because the interruption moment felt underserved: too many taps, too much configuration, too little help when attention is already thin.

App builders also need distribution taste alongside product taste. Shipping the app is one kind of signal. Explaining it, testing hooks, briefing creators, and feeding market feedback back into the product is a different kind of work.

AI helped most when it moved work forward. In the app, voice turns natural language into reminder actions. In the distribution studio, agents and extraction workflows turn messy research into drafts, briefs, and feedback. In development, agentic coding helped me move faster, but the product judgment still came from taste, lived use, and the constraints I wrote down for the agents.
