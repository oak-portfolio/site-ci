## The three-bin pause

You are holding a greasy takeout container, and all three bins look partly right.

That became the product brief.

[UNEP's 2024 outlook](https://www.unep.org/resources/global-waste-management-outlook-2024) says annual global waste costs could reach USD 640.3B by 2050 without urgent action. In California, one SB 1383 cost scenario models [450,000 businesses with at least five employees](https://www2.calrecycle.ca.gov/Docs/Web/115980). Those numbers explain the scale of the problem. They do not describe EcoSmartLoop adoption, savings, compliance, or impact.

Because we built it at SFBU in Fremont, the California context felt close. [SB 1383](https://calrecycle.ca.gov/organics/slcp/) turned waste sorting into real work for campuses, offices, restaurants, and city-scale programs around the Bay Area. Still, all of that work can come down to one person holding one confusing item.

Maybe it is a pizza box. Maybe it is a coffee cup lid, a takeout container, or a compostable-looking cup that is only compostable somewhere else. The bins are right there: <KeywordBadge>recycling</KeywordBadge>, <KeywordBadge>compost</KeywordBadge>, <KeywordBadge>landfill</KeywordBadge>. Someone may be waiting behind you. You pick the bin that feels least wrong.

The dashboard can come later. The report can come later. First, the person at the bin needs enough confidence to act and move on.

I built the product around that hesitation. The user should not have to become a waste-policy expert to throw something away correctly. The app should bring the decision support to the moment, explain why the recommendation makes sense, and make the next decision feel less random.

The scanner gave us a door into the problem. It did not create trust on its own. A label like "recycle" or "compost" helps only if the person believes it, understands it, and has a way to learn from it later.

The <KeywordBadge>AI agents</KeywordBadge> part only mattered if it helped after the label. Orbi had to explain the answer, cite the reason, remember the decision, and help the user do the next thing.

## The questions going in

I kept testing the capstone against four questions.

Would someone actually use it while standing at a bin? Could Orbi feel like an assistant inside the product instead of a green-themed chatbot? Could the whole thing work on a phone without turning into a separate native-app project? And would a business or institution care after the demo was over?

Those questions kept pulling me back from the easy version. A scanner that says "recycle" or "compost" is a good demo. The product I wanted had to explain, remember, teach, and show where the same confusion kept coming back.

I used those questions as a filter. If a feature did not make the answer faster, the reasoning clearer, the phone moment easier, or the organization view better, it probably did not belong.

From there, the work moved beyond <KeywordBadge>image classification</KeywordBadge>: cited guidance, rewards, history, quizzes, leaderboards, analytics, and admin surfaces.

## Where the direction came from

EcoSmartLoop did not start as a polished idea. We were kicking around capstone directions, including a multicultural-event scheduling app, before Glenn brought up waste sorting. The room changed a little when we talked about it. It was physical, confusing, local to California, and deep enough for both AI work and product judgment.

The team split became clear by doing the work. Frank Baah and Glenn Marvin Musoke primarily pushed <KeywordBadge>LEAF Engine</KeywordBadge> training and serving. Neelofar helped on frontend and analytics. I took the app around the classifier: scanner, API integration, Orbi AI, rewards, analytics, admin views, responsive UI, and the mobile shell.

## Why a California waste-sorting prototype was worth building

EcoSmartLoop came out of my SFBU M.S. capstone in Fremont, and <BrandIconLink provider="calrecycle" label="CalRecycle" href="https://calrecycle.ca.gov/organics/slcp/" /> gave the idea a real California operating context. [SB 1383](https://calrecycle.ca.gov/organics/slcp/collection/) means businesses and institutions have to think about education, container access, inspection, recordkeeping, and what happens when organic-waste containers are contaminated.

In practice, the problem was bigger than a recycling lookup. In an office, campus, or food-service environment, one wrong guess can become repeated behavior: confusing signage, contaminated bins, weak training, and no feedback after the decision. <BrandIconLink provider="calrecycle" label="CalRecycle contamination monitoring" href="https://calrecycle.ca.gov/organics/slcp/collection/contamination/" /> made that obvious to me. The hard part was not only knowing the class. It was helping people learn around the bin.

A CalRecycle SB 1383 cost scenario uses [450,000 California businesses with at least five employees](https://www2.calrecycle.ca.gov/Docs/Web/115980). The number stuck with me because it made the project feel large without pretending we had adoption. Nearly half a million businesses in one state is a reminder that waste sorting is a repeated workplace workflow, not a once-a-year climate pledge.

I also did not want the product to guilt people into caring more. People already try. The better green mission is more practical: make the right action easier and give people a reason to repeat it. The person gets a clearer answer at the bin. The product rewards better sorting. The organization sees what keeps going wrong. The admin has a reason to teach, incentivize, adjust the program, and keep better records for reporting later.

That mattered because the organization benefit is not only "make a prettier dashboard." CalRecycle has separate <BrandIconLink provider="calrecycle" label="recordkeeping guidance" href="https://www.calrecycle.ca.gov/organics/slcp/recordkeeping" /> for SB 1383, and California's [Cool California business guidance](https://coolcalifornia.arb.ca.gov/sustainability-improves-business-profitability) frames sustainability as part of long-term resilience, efficiency, and public accountability. I read those as a startup clue: if the product can improve behavior before the mistake, the organization has cleaner records, better training evidence, and a stronger story after the scan.

## What I had to make work

Once the team split was clear, my work became easier to describe. I was taking <KeywordBadge>LEAF Engine</KeywordBadge> out of the model notebook and putting it into a product someone could use while holding trash: capture the image, call the classifier safely, show the answer, handle weak confidence, save the scan, and connect the result to Orbi, rewards, history, and organization analytics.

Most of the judgment lived in the in-between states. A classifier can say "recycle." The app still has to answer the real user questions: how sure are we, why should I trust this, what if the answer is uncertain, and what changes after I scan?

## The decision loop

I did not want the app to stop at "take a picture, get a bin." That helps once. A real product needed memory.

The sequence became: scan, classify, explain, record, reward, learn, and make the next decision better. The <KeywordBadge>AI agents</KeywordBadge> part was Orbi. It could use sources and app context, make database-backed tool calls, generate UI, trigger app actions, expose uncertainty, and help the user complete the next step.

## LEAF Engine classified. Orbi explained.

EcoSmartLoop had two AI pieces, and I wanted the split to be obvious:

The simplest version: LEAF Engine decided what the item appeared to be, and Orbi helped the person understand what to do with that answer.

1. <KeywordBadge>LEAF Engine</KeywordBadge> did the photo read. It looked at the uploaded waste image and returned one of four outcomes: compost, recycle, landfill, or unknown, plus confidence/probability output. The shareable model story is simple: four classes, 8K+ image training context, MobileNetV2 transfer learning, 224x224 image input, <BrandIconLink provider="fastapi" label="FastAPI" /> serving, prediction logging, and a <KeywordBadge>low-confidence path</KeywordBadge>.

2. <KeywordBadge>Orbi AI</KeywordBadge> handled what happened after the label. I built it with <BrandIconLink provider="vercel" label="Vercel AI SDK" /> so the assistant could stay model-swappable instead of locked to one provider. Orbi could search and cite sources, call database-backed app tools, render structured UI, generate quiz/reward actions, and take product actions without making the user dig through a dashboard.

Today I would call Orbi the agent part of the product, but I do not mean that in a sci-fi way. It used context, sources, tool calls, generated UI, and product actions to move a user from "I think this is compost" to "here is the recommendation, here is why, here is what changed, and here is what to do next."

I started from <BrandIconLink provider="vercel" label="Vercel AI SDK v5" /> and Vercel AI Elements because the app needed more than a text box: streaming answers, source chips, visible tool progress, quiz actions, reward-aware responses, and app context. A generic prompt would have been faster. It also would have missed the whole reason Orbi existed: knowing where the user was in the product and what action could happen next.

## From scanner to behavior loop

V1 had the obvious pieces: scanner, classification result, dashboard, and basic analytics. It worked, but it felt thin. If the product only tells someone the answer once, why would they come back? And if people do not come back, why would an institution care beyond a one-time demo?

V2 is where it stopped feeling like only a camera flow. We integrated gift-card rewards, leaderboard features, quizzes, achievements, scan history, and admin controls. The question changed from "what bin is this?" to "how do we make the better choice worth repeating?"

A slogan can tell people recycling is important. That is not enough when someone is holding a messy container and the line behind them is moving. The product has to guide the person in the moment, give them feedback, make progress visible, and help the organization see where the confusion keeps coming back.

That direction matched what I was reading about pro-environment behavior. A [systematic review of behavior-change interventions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9486705/) points to feedback, goals, and gamified elements like challenges, points, badges, and leaderboards as useful patterns, while also warning that incentives alone are not magic. The [Campus Race to Zero Waste behavior guide](https://campusracetozerowaste.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/RM-Behavior-Change-Strategy-Guide-1.pdf) makes a similar practical point for recycling programs: rewards can help people try new behavior, especially when they are tied to performance.

That is the lane V2 moved into. In EcoSmartLoop, the reward was not supposed to be a random prize pasted on top of a green app. The scan created the learning moment. Orbi explained it. The leaderboard made participation visible. Tremendous gave us a gift-card path an admin could configure. We did not prove redemption at scale, but we did build the surfaces a campus or workplace would need to test whether incentives helped people come back and sort more carefully.

## The tradeoffs that made it real

Mobile was non-negotiable because the use case happens in front of a bin. Nobody wants to open a desktop dashboard to classify trash.

With a short capstone timeline, we chose <KeywordBadge>responsive web plus Capacitor</KeywordBadge> and focused on the iOS shell. That let us reuse the <BrandIconLink provider="nextjs" label="Next.js" /> app, auth behavior, API routes, database logic, and UI work. It also exposed the stuff browser demos hide: WebView cache behavior, login-session persistence, responsive edge cases, and real device differences.

It worked on phones in the end. Getting there was the painful part. If I were building the long-term mobile product, I would rather use <BrandIconLink provider="reactnative" label="React Native" /> or native SDKs than keep paying the WebView debugging tax.

## What the evidence actually showed

The $640.3B waste-cost projection and the 450K California-business cost scenario explain why I cared about the problem. EcoSmartLoop did not produce those numbers. It was a prototype aimed at one small part of that bigger operating mess: the decision before the item hits the wrong bin.

The prototype was still interesting without pretending it was already deployed. We had a working path from image classification to Orbi guidance, rewards, analytics, mobile shell, and admin views. Missing was the operating evidence a real institution would ask for: production pilots, repeated usage, compliance results, incentive-program fit, and final model-quality reporting.

For LEAF Engine, I can describe the public shape clearly: 8K+ images as training context, four output classes, confidence/probability output, and a serving path the product could call. Before I would make a stronger model-quality claim, I would want the exact split, thresholds, confusion matrix, precision, recall, and failure examples sitting beside it.

Then we made the decision that fit where we actually were: take the learning into industry first.

A serious waste-tech company needs more than a strong capstone. It needs pilots, operations knowledge, procurement cycles, compliance proof, model evaluation, customer support, deployment discipline, and a clear path through institutions. It would also need to prove the business case honestly: better sorting behavior, cleaner reporting, stronger public sustainability stories, and possible incentive, grant, rebate, or tax-credit opportunities where the organization actually qualifies. I would treat those as next-step business research, not as something the capstone already proved. We were early in our careers and wanted more experience before taking on that burden.

That decision belongs in the story. EcoSmartLoop was a serious team-built prototype, and it taught me how to think about AI products, incentives, and evidence. I am glad we treated it with ambition. I am also glad we did not pretend a capstone was already a company.

## What I took with me

EcoSmartLoop changed how I think about AI products.

Before this project, I cared mostly about making the AI work. After it, I cared more about the product around the AI: <KeywordBadge>context engineering</KeywordBadge>, tool design, source trust, user feedback, low-confidence handling, analytics, and the interface states that make a model usable.

It changed the way I build too. I started writing better source notes before asking an agent to help. I got stricter about logs, screenshots, design specs, and acceptance checks. I became more interested in the moment between model output and user action than in the model result alone.

I still use that lesson in my current work. After EcoSmartLoop, I built mini AI apps like Craftie and YouSoul faster because the workflow had changed: smaller context packets, better logs, reusable agent skills, clearer design specs, and more aggressive verification. I no longer treat AI as a blank chat box. I ask what context it has, what tools it can use, what it remembers, what needs to be checked, and how the user can see why the answer should be trusted.

The model mattered. Orbi made the answer usable. What I still care about most is the work around the model: make the prediction work, then make the decision clearer, safer, and easier to repeat.
